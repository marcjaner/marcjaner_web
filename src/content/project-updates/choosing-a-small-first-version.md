This is illustrative sample content shipped with the project journal feature. It shows
what a journal entry reads like — a reflective essay about a decision — and none of it
describes real work.

## The decision

The question I would be sitting with at this point is boring but real: how small can the
first version of a reading notebook be before it stops being a notebook at all?

Every version of this idea I have sketched grows the same way. It starts as "a list of
things I read, with a note each". Then someone — usually me — asks: what about tags?
What about a rating? What about a way to publish the notes, or to see what friends are
reading? Each addition is reasonable. Together they describe a product I would never
finish on weekends.

So the decision, in this example, is to cut everything except three things: an entry has
a title, a link, and a paragraph of what it made me think. No tags, no ratings, no
social anything.

## What got cut and why

Tags went first, which surprised me — they feel like the obvious feature. But the moment
I can file a note under "economics" or "design", I start writing for the taxonomy
instead of for myself, and the notes get shorter and more careful. The whole point is
that they are quick and a little messy.

Ratings went next. A number out of five flattens the one thing I actually want to keep,
which is the texture of the reaction. "This made me suspicious of its second argument"
is worth more to future me than "3".

The hardest cut was publishing. A public notebook is a forcing function — it makes you
write — but it also makes you perform. For the sample I am pretending the first version
is private, with the option to publish individual notes later once the habit exists.

## What I would try next

If this were real, the next step would be embarrassingly manual: keep notes in a single
Markdown file for two weeks and see whether the habit survives contact with a real
reading diet. If it does, the file itself tells you what the software needs to do —
search, maybe, or a monthly recap. If it does not, no amount of tooling was going to
save it anyway.

That is the trade-off this entry is illustrating: choosing a version small enough that
its failure would be informative. A big first version can fail quietly — too many
features, never shipped, conclusion unclear. A small one fails honestly and cheaply.
